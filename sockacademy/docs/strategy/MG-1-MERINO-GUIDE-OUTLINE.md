## Section 1: What "Merino" Actually Means

**Target length:** 300–400 words
**Function:** Establish that "Merino" is not a protected designation and that most brands exploit the ambiguity.

### Key Authority Claim
> "There is no legal requirement for a product labeled 'Merino' to contain wool of any specific micron count. The term is a breed designation, not a quality standard. Most brands use it as the latter."

### Required Content
- Merino is a sheep breed — specifically Ovis aries merinoicus — selectively bred over 300 years for fine fiber
- New Zealand South Island conditions (cool nights, high-altitude pasture, low stress) produce the finest fiber; terroir matters in wool as it does in wine
- "Merino" appears on products containing wool from 16μ (ultra-fine, luxury) to 24μ+ (coarse, mass market) — without disclosure of which
- The ZQ certification program by New Zealand Merino Company is the only current traceability protocol that ties a garment back to a specific farm

### What Not to Say
- Do not name specific brands that misuse the term
- Do not use "premium" or "luxury" as adjectives — describe the facts and let the reader conclude

### Sourcing Phase Use
When discussing fiber sourcing with manufacturers: reference Section 1 to establish that you know the distinction between breed origin and quality designation. Ask suppliers to confirm their Merino source country and micron distribution, not just "Merino" as a label.

## Section 2: The Micron Standard — Why 18.5μ Is the Threshold

**Target length:** 400–500 words
**Function:** Explain the only number that matters in Merino quality. Establish our standard and why it is non-negotiable.

### Key Authority Claim
> "18.5 microns is not a marketing choice. It is the threshold below which wool fiber cannot consistently activate the mechanoreceptors in human skin that register itch. We do not source above it."

### Required Content
- What a micron is: 1/1000th of a millimeter. For scale: a human hair is approximately 70 microns.
- The prickling/itch threshold: Fiber ends protruding from fabric above approximately 30μ activate nociceptors in skin. Below 18.5μ, fiber is too fine to trigger this response in most people.
- Micron classification reference table (do not frame as our own — frame as industry standard):
  - ≤15μ: Ultra-fine / Superfine (rare, expensive, limited production)
  - 15.1–18.5μ: Fine (Academy Grade minimum threshold)
  - 18.6–20μ: Medium-fine (common in "premium" consumer products)
  - 20.1–24μ: Medium (standard commercial Merino)
  - 24μ+: Coarse (not appropriate for next-to-skin use)
- How micron count is measured: OFDA (Optical Fibre Diameter Analyser) — not a manual process. Certificates are issued per lot.
- Why micron count declines with age: older Merino sheep produce coarser fiber. ZQ certification includes animal age management.
- Why sub-18.5μ is not automatically better: below 15μ, fiber becomes fragile and requires synthetic reinforcement at high ratios to maintain durability.

### What Not to Say
- Do not claim 18.5μ is medically necessary for anyone — it is a comfort threshold based on mechanoreceptor research, not a clinical standard
- Do not position this as our proprietary discovery — it is established textile science

### Sourcing Phase Use
This section becomes the technical anchor of any supplier conversation. Ask for: OFDA test certificates per batch, not per fiber source. The batch micron distribution matters — a "18.5μ average" with a wide distribution still contains high-micron outliers.

## Section 3: How the Fiber Actually Works

**Target length:** 400–500 words
**Function:** Explain the mechanism behind Merino's performance claims. Make it structural, not marketing.

### Key Authority Claim
> "Merino wool manages moisture not by wicking (a surface behavior) but through active hygroscopic absorption at the fiber level. This is a structural difference. It is why Merino remains comfortable when wet in a way that synthetic fiber does not."

### Required Content

**Temperature regulation (the mechanism):**
- Wool fiber has a cortex (inner protein structure) that absorbs up to 35% of its own weight in moisture vapor before the outer surface feels wet
- As moisture is absorbed, a small exothermic reaction releases heat — warming the wearer in cold conditions
- As moisture evaporates, endothermic cooling occurs — the fiber actively participates in thermoregulation
- This is not wicking (moving moisture from one surface to another). It is absorption and phase-change.

**Odor resistance (the mechanism):**
- Wool fiber scales have a negative charge that traps positively charged odor-causing bacteria away from the skin surface
- Natural lanolin content (partially retained in Superwash-treated Merino) inhibits bacterial colonization at the fiber scale level
- This is why a Merino sock worn for 12 hours can be re-worn. A cotton or synthetic sock cannot.

**Durability (the mechanism):**
- Wool fiber can be bent approximately 20,000 times before breaking
- Cotton fiber: approximately 3,200 cycles
- Standard synthetic (nylon): approximately 75 cycles before fiber fatigue (nylon recovers; it does not break as quickly as this implies — but it fatigues)
- This is why Merino requires nylon reinforcement in heel and toe zones: not because Merino is weak, but because those zones see concentrated, repetitive mechanical stress that exceeds the fiber's resilience

### What Not to Say
- Do not claim odor resistance is permanent or absolute
- Do not reference specific lab studies by name without verified citation — write "research in textile science has established" rather than citing papers you cannot verify
- "Antimicrobial" is a regulated claim in some markets — do not use it

## Section 4: Why Construction Determines Whether Merino Performs

**Target length:** 350–450 words
**Function:** Establish that fiber quality is necessary but not sufficient. The sock's construction determines whether the fiber's properties are expressed or wasted.

### Key Authority Claim
> "An 18.5-micron yarn knitted on a 120-needle machine pills faster than a 19.5-micron yarn knitted on a 200-needle machine. Micron count without construction standard is an incomplete specification."

### Required Content

**Needle count:**
- Higher needle count = more stitches per inch = finer gauge fabric
- Fine Merino fiber requires high needle count to be knitted without creating loose loops that cause pilling
- Academy Grade minimum: 200 needles. Standard commercial: 120–144.
- Practical implication: on a 120-needle machine, 18.5μ Merino will pill within 3–5 wash cycles. On a 200-needle machine, it will not.

**Nylon reinforcement:**
- 15–20% nylon in heel and toe zones is not a fiber compromise — it is engineering
- Pure Merino in high-wear zones fails within months of regular use
- Correct ratio: Merino primary + nylon structural = durability without sacrificing hand feel
- Incorrect ratio: >25% nylon throughout = the sock no longer performs as Merino

**Linked toe seam:**
- Looped toe seam: a raised ridge across the widest joint of the foot. Creates a pressure point over 8+ hours of wear.
- Linked toe seam: closed stitch-by-stitch, leaving a smooth internal profile. Requires specialized machinery.
- At fine Merino gauge, the linked seam is doubly important — the fiber is soft enough that a looped seam is the only tactile irritant remaining.

**Superwash treatment:**
- Raw Merino felts when machine washed — the scales interlock and the fabric shrinks
- Superwash removes the outermost scale layer (chlorine-Hercosett or bio-enzymatic process) to allow machine washing
- Bio-enzymatic Superwash is preferable from an environmental standpoint
- A sock that cannot be machine washed is not viable for daily wear — Superwash is not optional at this price point

### What Not to Say
- Do not imply linked toe seam is rare (it is not rare at premium tier — it is standard at that tier)
- Do not overclaim Superwash durability — it reduces some moisture management properties marginally

## Section 5: The ZQ Standard — Traceability as a Guarantee

**Target length:** 300–350 words
**Function:** Explain why we require ZQ certification and why traceability is the real value — not just the ethical signal.

### Key Authority Claim
> "ZQ certification is not an environmental badge. It is a traceability protocol. We require it because a manufacturer who cannot tell you which farm produced the wool cannot guarantee what you received."

### Required Content
- ZQ is a certification program managed by The New Zealand Merino Company
- It certifies three things: fiber quality (micron count per lot), animal welfare (mulesing-free, standards above NZ law), and environmental stewardship (land management, carbon, water)
- Traceability chain: farm → shearing → grading → spinning mill → knitting factory → garment
- Why traceability matters for quality: fiber from different farms in the same region can vary by 2–3 microns. Without lot-level documentation, "18.5μ average" is a batch claim, not a consistent fiber claim.
- Why traceability matters for brand: when a customer asks "where did this wool come from?" — we can answer specifically. Most brands cannot.
- Alternative certifications: Responsible Wool Standard (RWS) covers animal welfare but not fiber quality. OEKO-TEX Standard 100 covers chemical safety but not fiber origin. ZQ is the only current standard that addresses all three simultaneously.

### What Not to Say
- Do not overstate ZQ as the only ethical wool program — acknowledge alternatives exist
- Do not frame this as environmentalism first — frame it as quality assurance first, with ethical benefits as consequential

## Sourcing Phase Integration

This guide functions as a credentialing document in manufacturer conversations. When sent alongside the Sourcing Brief:

1. **It signals knowledge.** A buyer who has published a 2,000-word technical guide on micron standards does not get sold generic product.
2. **It sets the baseline.** Manufacturers who read it self-select. Those who cannot meet Section 2–4 standards will not waste your time responding.
3. **It creates a reference point.** In supplier calls, you can say: "Our published standard requires ZQ certification, 200-needle construction, and linked toe seam. Can you confirm your capability against each of those three items?" — not "we want something nice."
4. **It establishes that you will know if they cut corners.** A brand that publishes fiber science does not accept vague assurances.
