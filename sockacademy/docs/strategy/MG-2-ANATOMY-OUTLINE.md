## Purpose Statement

MG-1 established what the fiber is and why 18.5μ Merino performs the way it does. MG-2 answers the next question a technically literate reader will ask: fiber quality is necessary, but how is it actually built into a sock — and how would anyone verify that our construction claims are real rather than marketing?

This guide converts the theoretical material science from MG-1 into physical, inspectable specification. Where MG-1 talked about fiber, MG-2 talks about geometry, gauge, and mechanical engineering. The core argument: "engineered," not "soft," is the correct word for what we sell.

## Section 2: The Linked Toe — Why the Seam Is the First Thing to Check

**Target length:** 350–400 words
**Function:** Most tactile, most immediately verifiable claim in the guide. Should function as the "aha" section a reader can act on immediately.

### Key Authority Claim
> "Turn any sock inside out and press your thumb across the toe closure. A looped seam presents as a raised ridge — you will feel it instantly. A linked seam presents as a flat, continuous surface. This is not subjective. It is a pass/fail test anyone can perform in five seconds."

### Required Content
- **Looped toe seam:** The standard, low-cost method — a chain-stitch closure sewn across the open toe after knitting, producing a ridge that sits directly over the widest joint of the foot (the metatarsophalangeal joint)
- **Linked toe seam:** Stitch-by-stitch closure using a linking machine (or hand-linking at the highest tier) that joins each individual loop from the final knitted row — producing a flat, seamless profile
- Why this matters more at fine gauge: at 18.5μ, the fiber itself is soft enough that a looped seam becomes the single remaining source of irritation across 8+ hours of wear — the "last mile" problem of an otherwise premium product
- Manufacturing cost reality: linking is slower, requires specialized machinery and trained operators — which is precisely why most mass-market and even mid-tier brands skip it
- **The verification instruction:** invite the reader to check their existing socks this way — this is the single most shareable, screenshot-able claim in the entire guide

## Section 3: The Heel Pocket — Structured Cup vs. Flat Construction

**Target length:** 300–350 words
**Function:** Explain the second most significant construction differentiator — one most readers have never consciously noticed but will recognize once described.

### Key Authority Claim
> "A flat-constructed heel is a tube with a fold. A structured heel pocket is knitted with short-row shaping to form a three-dimensional cup that matches the geometry of a human heel. One requires the sock to stretch and hold its position against gravity and friction for eight hours. The other does not need to."

### Required Content
- Flat/tube heel: simplest, cheapest construction — no shaping, relies entirely on overall sock elasticity to stay in place, common source of slippage and bunching inside the shoe
- Structured heel pocket: uses short-row (turn-and-wrap) knitting technique to build a fitted three-dimensional pocket — the same technique used in hand-knitting heel turns for a century, mechanized at scale
- Why this matters for all-day wear: a slipping heel pocket is the primary cause of a sock "walking down" into the shoe over the course of a day
- Verification instruction: hold the sock heel up to a cupped hand — a structured heel visibly curves; a flat heel does not

## Section 4: The Arch Zone — Graduated Structure, Not Medical Compression

**Target length:** 300–350 words
**Function:** Address the "Foot Health" claim directly and carefully — this is the section most exposed to regulatory scrutiny (per PRIVATE_LABEL_ROADMAP.md Phase 0 gap analysis), so language discipline matters most here.

### Key Authority Claim
> "The arch band is a structural knit variation — a tighter rib density across the midfoot — not a medical compression garment. We do not claim therapeutic effect. We claim that a structurally supported arch band holds its position on the foot better than a uniform-tension sock, which is a mechanical fact, not a health claim."

### Required Content
- What graduated arch structure actually is: a change in rib density (stitches per inch) across the midfoot section, produced by adjusting tension on the knitting machine — not an added material or medical device
- What it is not: a compression sock in the medical sense (graduated mmHg compression for circulation), which is a regulated product category we do not enter
- Function: reduces fabric migration and bunching under the arch during motion — a fit and durability benefit, described as such
- **Compliance note (internal):** this section must never use "supports circulation," "reduces swelling," or "medical-grade" — see PRIVATE_LABEL_ROADMAP.md Phase 0 regulatory gap

## Section 5: The Cuff and Rib — Why Most Cuffs Fail Within a Year

**Target length:** 250–300 words
**Function:** Close with the most common visible failure point in a mass-market sock, and how construction prevents it.

### Key Authority Claim
> "A cuff that loosens is not a fiber failure. It is an elastane ratio and rib-density failure. The two variables are independent of the wool used, which is why a $8 cotton sock and a $40 Merino sock can fail identically at the cuff within the same six months."

### Required Content
- 2×1 or 3×1 rib knit minimum — the alternating knit/purl structure that gives a cuff its elastic recovery
- Elastane percentage (2–5%) integrated into the rib, not just at the very top edge — full-cuff elastane vs. top-band-only elastane, the latter being a common cost-cutting method that fails within months
- Dimensional stability requirement (≤5% shrinkage) directly protects cuff integrity — a cuff that shrinks disproportionately to the leg loses its fit even if the elastic itself hasn't failed
- Verification instruction: stretch the cuff of a new sock and release it — it should return to original shape without visible slack. Repeat mentally with a sock the reader already owns that has "gone soft" at the top.
